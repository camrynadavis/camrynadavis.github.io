***Background***<br>
Sports specialization is extensive training in a sport, designed to enhance performance and expose athletes to recruiters and scouting opportunities through high-level competition. Studies have shown that a majority of athletes between 12-18 years old believe that sports specialization increases their chances of being on a college team; furthermore, “highly specialized” athletes were twice as likely to believe they would receive a scholarship to play their sport than “low specialized” athletes (Brooks, Post, & Trigsted 2018). Compared to middle-income and high-income households, lower-income parents are most likely to weigh the most importance on obtaining an athletic scholarship through sports specialization; additionally, they are the most likely to sign their children up for sports specialization to keep them out of trouble (Kroshus et al., 2021). Therefore, this suggests that stakes for youth athletes coming from lower-income households are much higher than those coming from wealthier households. With youth sports spending increasing by 55% since 2010, and 46% between 2019 and 2025, this poses a significant problem for athletes coming from lower-income communities, as many families are not able to afford the required expenses. Costs for specialization vary depending on sport, but typically include travel, AAU/club team registration, equipment, private training, and camp fees (Ekmekjian, Snyder, & Snyder 2025). With basketball being the most common AAU sport, and one of the four major sports in the United States, this project seeks to explore whether college basketball recruitment is being impacted by rising youth sports costs.

***Analysis***<br>
To explore patterns between income and recruitment, roster data from NCAA Division I basketball rosters between 2020-2025 and data for girls aged 15-19 in the ACS 5-Year Estimates were used. Using KMeans clustering, three categories were classified: high income recruiting, low income recruiting, and low-density area recruiting. The main finding showed that for the 2020-2021 season, over 50% of athletes on a roster for a Division I basketball team came from lower-income cities, while a little less than 48.5% came from higher-income cities. Since then, there has been a consistent decline for athletes coming from lower-income cities, with a total shift in majority taking place in 2023. In the 2024-2025 season, athletes coming from higher-income cities rose to 50.59%, with athletes coming from lower-income cities decreasing to around 48%. Using linear regression, it was forecasted that the percentage of athletes coming from higher-income cities would rise to 52.01% by 2028, and those coming from lower-income cities would decrease to 47.76%. The remaining percentage is athletes coming from low density, often rural areas with a small population of the specified age group. 

This finding directly correlates with the rise of youth sports expenses between 2019 and 2025; as participation fees increase, the recruitment in lower-income communities decreases. To further support this finding, two different variables were created measuring the number of unique recruits coming from each city and the rate at which they are recruited. The first was Recruitment Density, calculated to normalize the varying city populations and discover how many unique recruits per 10,000 came from each city. This metric and poverty rate percentages found using the ACS were then used to calculate the Scouting Desert Score, which was designed to locate areas in the United States with high populations of the specified age group, but low numbers of recruits. A scatterplot visualizing average household median income and scouting desert score showed a higher concentration of lower-income cities with higher desert scores. Additionally, among the top twenty cities with the highest desert scores, twelve were classified as lower-income cities – eight of the twelve were classified as high poverty cities (> 20% poverty rate), and four as extreme poverty (> 40% poverty rate). 

Data also showed that Black and Hispanic recruits were more likely to be recruited from lower-income cities; during the 2024-2025 season, the percentage of Black and Hispanic recruits in higher-income cities was 17.29% cumulatively and 25.14% cumulatively in lower-income cities. Black recruits specifically were more than twice as likely to be recruited in lower-income areas, reflected in the increase from 5.82% to 12.04% increase by city income level. 

***Limitations***<br>
There are several limitations to this study. This study measures recruitment density using the general population of girls aged 15-19 in the United States, rather than just those that play basketball on a school or club/AAU team. Therefore, this study is more so a reflection of the scope of scouting opportunities rather than an individual’s chances of playing Division I. This study also does not account for the number of players who do not play for their hometown school, and instead attend private institutions or schools with better resources to increase their chances of gaining scouting exposure. Lastly, this study focuses on the 50 states in the US and the District of Columbia, so territories such as Puerto Rico, Virgin Islands, Guam, etc. were omitted due to inconsistencies in ACS data reporting. Also omitted were recruits from unincorporated areas and neighborhoods with missing socioeconomic data due to how they were reported in the ‘City’ column of the ACS; the justification for the omission is that over twenty-five thousand unique recruits remained, which was a sufficient sample size to this project.

***Recommendations***<br>
While COVID stalled youth sports in 2020, youth sports spending reached record highs in 2023. The rapid increase in demand revealed how much families were willing to pay for their child’s development, garnering attention from private equity firms and transforming youth sports into a monetized business model. For many athletes, youth sports are essential for the pursuit of hobbies, socialization, and opportunities to secure scholarships for higher education. As the cost of participation increases, these opportunities become less inclusive, creating financial barriers that disproportionately shut out lower-income and Black and Hispanic athletes. To make these opportunities more inclusive, there needs to be a greater investment in community-based strategies. This includes a greater effort towards training volunteer coaches, establishing second-hand equipment exchange programs to alleviate equipment costs, and building relationships with local businesses and organizations to gain sponsorships.
